Why does an empty office have to become another apartment?

A proposal for a different kind of home in San Francisco — private rooms, real shared space, and a community that isn't sorted by who you are.

Find your path → See the idea
The reframe

"Apartment" is the default answer. It doesn't have to be the only one.

San Francisco has vacant office floors and a housing shortage at the same time. The instinct is to convert one into the other, using the same product we've always built. That's one option — not the only one.

San Francisco just committed $610.5M through a new downtown financing district specifically to fund office-to-housing conversions. About 50 buildings qualify. Nobody's proposed the shared-community version yet.

The model
One private module. Five shared ones. The ratio is the product.

Your own room. Everything else, shared.

Locked door, your space, your rules. Kitchen, bathroom, lounge, rooftop — shared with people actually building a life in this city, same as you. Some amenities are bundled in. Others — a gym, a coworking room — you add only if you'll use them, so nobody subsidizes space they'll never touch.

Who this is for

Not a building for one kind of person. A building for a city's worth of them.

New graduate

You don't have to choose between living at home and living alone. There's a third option — and it's yours.

Their parents

You want them independent. You also want them safe, and around good people. What if you didn't have to choose?

An aging parent

You've spent a lifetime building your own life on your own terms. This is a bigger version of that — with people around, on your schedule.

New to the city

You already did the hard part — you moved. This is where you plant your feet and start building something.

Plumbing and kitchens need to be added where offices don't already have them, and egress/occupancy code has to be met for residential use. What's often already in place — structure, envelope, elevators, and in older buildings, operable windows — is the expensive part in ground-up construction. Older, smaller-floorplate buildings (the ones the city already flags as best suited for conversion) are also the ones least suited to standard full-apartment layouts, which is exactly where this model has room to work.
Zoning, occupancy/egress code, and utility retrofit requirements are real barriers — this isn't a simple switch to flip. That's exactly why this starts as a demand-test and conversation, not a construction plan.
Deliberately mixed by age and income — students, service workers, retirees, professionals. That's lower exposure to any single market segment softening than a building leased to one demographic.
1455 Market Street is a strong candidate. A podium-level floor there runs about 66,000 square feet with 16-foot ceilings — tall enough to consider sleeping lofts within private rooms, adding usable volume without adding footprint. It has two existing kitchen/break areas in different zones of the floor, which map naturally onto two separate resident neighborhoods, each anchored by plumbing that's already in place. It also has a separate street entry and dedicated parking, meaning a pilot could run independently of the rest of the building's tenants.

The opposite — the model is deliberately mixed by age and income by design, not segmented. That mix is the point, not a side effect.
Two things: design and operational standard (consistent materials, real maintenance, funded staffing from day one — the same discipline that turned a rundown SF motel into one of the country's largest boutique hotel brands) and a documented, evenly-enforced community agreement rather than any judgment-based admission process. Screening is objective and behavior-based, not identity-based.
Standard, legal screening (income, references, credit) at the door, plus a written community agreement, consistent enforcement with real consequences, and funded upkeep from day one. Source-of-income discrimination is illegal in San Francisco and this model doesn't rely on it — standards are behavioral and documented, applied the same to every resident.
Zoning for residential use, occupancy/egress code built for offices rather than homes, and utility retrofit costs. None of these are unique to this model — they're the same barriers any office conversion faces, and the city's existing Downtown Revitalization Financing District was built specifically to help clear them.
Nothing beyond what the existing conversion framework already offers — this is a proposal to use a program the city has already funded, differently, not a request for a new one.
One example worth naming directly: 1455 Market Street. The city already leases more than four floors there, roughly 157,000 square feet, with an option to buy the entire building. A single podium-level floor in that building runs about 66,000 square feet, has a separate street entry and dedicated parking independent of the rest of the building, and could operate as a self-contained pilot without disrupting other tenants. This isn't a hypothetical site — it's a building the city already has a financial relationship with.
